## In two minutes

You have been pitched "digitise or die" for fifteen years — by aggregators, by every OTA
representative, and by three channel-manager salespeople this year. It is reasonable to file new
urgency with the rest.

So before we ask you for anything, here is the list of claims about AI in hospitality that the
evidence does not support. Several of them would have made this page considerably more persuasive.

1. **"AI agents will book most travel within a year or two."** Under 1% of hotel bookings come
   through AI today, and only 8% of travellers are comfortable letting it book at all.
2. **"AI browsers are the next channel."** OpenAI launched one in October 2025 and shut it down in
   August 2026.
3. **"Our AI resolves 85–90% of guest queries."** Airbnb, with more travel conversation data than
   any hotel vendor will ever have, reaches 45%.
4. **"AI revenue management delivers +19% RevPAR."** Vendor-reported, never independently
   validated. The defensible range is 5–15%.
5. **"AI will cut your staffing costs by 20%."** Those savings were calculated at European wages
   and shrink roughly tenfold in India.
6. **"Add an llms.txt file and AI will find you."** No major engine uses it for retrieval.
7. **"Agentic payments will transform hotel revenue in 2027."** Visa had processed "hundreds" of
   agent transactions as of December 2025, and India is in no rollout.
8. **"This vendor got 17% of bookings via AI in 60 days."** Vendor-published, unaudited, no
   methodology.
9. **"Google's AI Max will lift your bookings."** Google claims +14% conversions; an independent
   study of 250 campaigns found a 16% *worse* cost per acquisition.

If someone quotes you one of these, ask for the source. The answer will tell you a lot about who
you are dealing with.

## The detail

### 1 · Agents are about to book everything

Booking Holdings confirmed in August 2026 that AI-driven bookings are **under 1% of volume**. Only
about **11% of travel companies** have an agent technically capable of pricing and completing a
booking in real time. On the demand side, Expedia's study with YouGov found **8% of travellers
comfortable letting AI book**, Skift found 2% would allow it without sign-off, and Accenture
measured 7% for fully autonomous booking.

The constraint is trust about money, and it moves slowly.

### 2 · The AI browser

OpenAI launched the Atlas browser in **October 2025** and **shut it down on 9 August 2026** — under
a year later, never having dented Chrome's share. An entire predicted channel appeared and vanished
inside ten months.

Keep that story in mind the next time anyone is confident about a channel that does not exist yet.

### 3 · Ninety per cent resolution

**Airbnb's AI resolves about 45%** of the inquiries it handles, up from 33% two quarters earlier,
cutting support cost per booking by 16% year on year.

When a vendor claims double that, they are redefining the denominator — usually by counting only
the simplest message types, or by counting a deflection as a resolution. Ask precisely what counts
as "resolved", and what happens to the rest.

### 4 · The revenue management number

There is **no independent validation of any AI revenue-management RevPAR claim.** The widely quoted
"+19%" is vendor-reported. A 5 to 15% band is defensible, and even that is measured against hotels
doing no yield management at all — which, to be fair, describes a great many independents.

### 5 · The staffing saving

This is the one that matters most in India, and it is arithmetic rather than opinion.

A housekeeping optimisation tool with a payroll-verified saving of 2.5 minutes per room is worth
roughly **€13,800 a year at €16 an hour**. At Indian wages the same saving is worth about
**€900 to €1,400**. Nearly every hospitality AI business case drops by about a factor of ten when
it crosses into India.

Now compare the price. A complete property management system for a 50-room Indian hotel costs
**₹3,000 to ₹6,000 a month**. A Western AI guest-messaging tool at €4 per room per month is about
**₹20,000 a month**. Some Western support AI, priced per resolution, works out near **₹44,000**.

Western hospitality AI frequently costs **two to ten times an Indian property's entire software
budget**, to deliver a saving computed at wages ten times higher than the ones you actually pay.

The honest argument for AI in Indian hotel operations is not labour savings. It is **continuity** —
against 30 to 40% annual attrition and properties running at 60 to 70% of intended staffing, the
value is that the work still happens when the person leaves.

Also worth knowing: Accor's widely quoted remark about replacing 30 to 40% of jobs referred
explicitly to **corporate** roles. **No chain has demonstrated on-property headcount reductions.**

### 6 · llms.txt

A file some people add to websites in the belief that it helps AI systems understand them. As of
2026 **no major engine uses it as a retrieval or ranking mechanism**. It costs nothing, so publish
one if you like — but schema.org structured data and a complete Google Business Profile are what
actually do the work.

### 7 · Agentic payments in 2027

As of December 2025, Visa had processed **only "hundreds"** of agent transactions. Mastercard's
Agent Pay has not launched in India at any stage. India's own delegated-payment rails cap out at
**₹5,000 per transaction**, against hotel bookings of ₹18,000 and up.

### 8 · The 17% case study

A widely circulated figure claiming a property reached 17% of bookings through AI channels within
60 days. It is vendor-published and unaudited, with no methodology attached. We mention it because
you will encounter it, and because noticing who repeats it without qualification is useful
information.

### 9 · AI Max

Google claims its automated search product delivers +14% conversions at similar cost. An
independent study of 250 campaigns found roughly +13% revenue but a **16% worse cost per
acquisition**, and 84% of surveyed advertisers reported neutral or negative results.

This is the cleanest example of a vendor number and a field number pointing in opposite directions.

### Three things nobody knows

Finally, in the same spirit — gaps where we would rather say "unknown" than guess:

- **There is no credible survey of AI adoption among Indian hotels.** Anyone quoting a percentage
  is extrapolating.
- **Indian chains have disclosed no measured AI operations outcomes.** When you ask what Taj or
  Lemon Tree are getting from this, the honest answer is that they have not said.
- **There are no measured outcomes for AI review-response tools.** That category sells on
  plausibility alone.

### So what is actually true

Stripped of the exaggeration, the case is narrower and, we would argue, more useful:

> For the next eighteen to twenty-four months, AI changes **who gets shortlisted**, not **who takes
> the payment**. The work that wins the shortlist is cheap, available today, and something five out
> of six hotels have not done.

That claim survives every source above, and it survives the frontier slipping by two years.
