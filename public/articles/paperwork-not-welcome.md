## In two minutes

Check-in is two jobs wearing one uniform.

One is clerical: collecting identity, verifying a document, capturing a signature, filing a
statutory return, taking the balance payment. The other is hospitality: the first impression, the
sense of having arrived somewhere.

Technology has been sold to hotels as though these were the same job — which is why self-service
check-in so often disappoints. It automates the greeting and leaves the paperwork.

The evidence says do exactly the opposite. About **78% of guests say they want self-service
options**, but real adoption of mobile check-in at mature deployments runs at **25 to 40%** — plan
for one guest in three, not four in five. And on the human side the findings are blunt: **81% of
guests find AI-led welcomes emotionally inauthentic**, 76% raise privacy concerns about voice
systems, around 70% prefer a person, and **59% of hoteliers — most strongly those already using
AI — say check-in should stay human-led**.

For a retreat or a wellness property this is not even a trade-off. The arrival *is* the product.

Meanwhile, two genuinely useful things changed in India this year, and neither involves AI at all:

- **Aadhaar verifiable credentials became legally valid identification** in April 2026, and in June
  2026 **FHRAI advised member hotels to verify guests that way instead of keeping photocopies**.
- **Form C** — the 24-hour filing for every foreign guest — remains a hand-typed chore at most
  properties, and it is pure clerical automation, no AI required.

## The detail

### What changed in the law, and why it helps you

A UIDAI circular dated 30 April 2026 recognised credentials presented through the Aadhaar app as
legally valid identification. On 12 June 2026, FHRAI advised hotels to verify guests this way
rather than retaining photocopies. Hotels register as offline verification entities through UIDAI's
portal.

Three benefits follow, and only one of them is speed:

1. **A faster arrival**, obviously.
2. **A verified identity** rather than a photocopy, which proves nothing and never did.
3. **The disappearance of a drawer full of identity documents** — which stops being merely untidy
   and starts being a liability when India's data protection rules take full effect in May 2027.

That third point is the one most owners have not thought about. Every photocopied Aadhaar card in
a filing cabinet is personal data you are holding without a retention policy, a consent record, or
a deletion schedule.

### Form C: a solved problem still being done by hand

Under the Immigration and Foreigners Act 2025, every property hosting a foreign national must file
details within 24 hours of arrival. Nepal and Bhutan citizens are exempt; OCI cardholders are now
included.

This is the clearest automation win available to a retreat, and it requires **no AI in the modern
sense whatsoever**. Reading a passport's machine-readable zone has been solved for two decades.

Consider what actually happens today at a property with thirty foreign guests arriving for a course
start date: someone sits at a screen and types thirty forms into a government portal, against a
24-hour legal deadline, with criminal liability attached to the owner if it slips. Nobody designed
that process. It accumulated.

What automation can honestly do here: capture the document once at online check-in, extract the
fields, assemble the filing, flag what is missing, and track what has been submitted and
acknowledged. The FRRO portal has no public interface for submission, so a human still presses the
final button — but the typing, the chasing and the tracking all go away.

### Where the line sits

We would put automation on everything before the guest reaches the door, and nothing after it:

**Automate:** document collection, identity verification, health and dietary forms, consent
capture, signature, balance payment, arrival-time coordination, transfer booking, and the Form C
assembly.

**Do not automate:** the greeting, the orientation, the first conversation about what the guest
came for. For a wellness property in particular, this is the moment the guest is paying for.

The research supports this split rather than contradicting it. Guests are not rejecting technology
at arrival; they are rejecting the *replacement of the person*. A guest who completed everything on
their phone the night before and is met at the door by someone who already knows their name, their
dietary needs and why they came has had both — and neither was possible with a clipboard.

### A realistic expectation

Budget for **one guest in three** completing online check-in, not everyone. Adoption is better
among younger and international guests, and better still when the link arrives on WhatsApp rather
than by email, with a clear reason attached ("complete this and your room will be ready when you
arrive").

The one in three who complete it are the ones who would otherwise have taken the longest at the
desk, because they are the ones with passports, visas and forms.
